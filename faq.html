Whole-Home Repiping & Pipe Health

1. How do I know if my historic Arvada or Denver home needs a whole-home repipe?

If your home was built before 1965 in older areas like Park Hill, Wash Park, or Olde Town Arvada, it likely contains galvanized iron pipes. Over decades, these pipes corrode from the inside out. Clear warning signs include a noticeable drop in water pressure, red or rusty water when you first turn on the tap, frequent pinhole leaks in crawlspaces, or a metallic taste in your drinking water. Replacing these with modern Uponor PEX Type A or Type L Copper completely fixes these issues.

2. What is PEX Type A and why is it better for freezing Colorado temperatures?

PEX Type A is manufactured using the Engel cold-expansion method, which gives it the highest cross-linking density and thermal shape memory. If water freezes inside a PEX-A pipe, the material can expand up to three times its normal diameter and shrink back to its original shape without splitting or cracking. Read our PEX-A vs. PEX-B guide to see why it is vastly superior to rigid copper or cheaper PEX-B lines during extreme Colorado winter drops.

3. How long does a whole-home repipe take, and do we have to move out?

A standard residential whole-home repipe typically takes our team between 1 to 2 working days to complete. You absolutely do not have to move out. We ensure your home remains livable by running new lines systematically and restoring your main water connection at the end of every single workday.

4. Why is my water pressure so low in my older Denver home?

Low water pressure in older Denver properties is usually caused by internal rust accumulation inside original galvanized steel pipes. As these lines rust from the inside out, the internal diameter shrinks down to the size of a straw, choking off the flow of water to your showers and taps. The only permanent fix is replacing the old internal premise piping with clean modern lines.

5. What is polybutylene piping and why is it considered a ticking time bomb?

Polybutylene is a flexible gray plastic pipe widely installed in home construction from 1978 through 1995. Municipal water treatment chloramines cause this material to become brittle and micro-fracture from the inside out, leading to sudden, catastrophic bursts. Learn how to identify it on our polybutylene replacement guide. Colorado insurance companies frequently deny claims or drop policies when active polybutylene is present.

6. Will a whole-home repipe increase my home's resale value in Denver?

Yes, absolutely. Home buyers, mortgage underwriters, and property inspectors view outdated galvanized, Kitec, or polybutylene plumbing as major structural liabilities. Upgrading your property to a brand-new, Master Plumber-certified Uponor PEX-A or Type L Copper system completely removes this obstacle, speeds up home sales, and directly boosts your market appraisal valuation. See real examples in our local project case studies.

Water Heaters & Water Chemistry

7. Why does hard water destroy water heaters in Castle Rock and Aurora?

Castle Rock and Aurora have high mineral contents (calcium and magnesium). When water is heated, these minerals crystallize and settle to the bottom of standard tank water heaters, creating a thick scale barrier. This barrier forces the system to run hotter, causing the tank metal to fatigue, overheat, and burst prematurely. We recommend regular flushing or upgrading to high-efficiency water heating units built to handle local hard water tables.

8. Should I upgrade to a tankless water heater in Centennial or stick with a traditional tank?

Tankless water heaters are highly recommended for Front Range homes due to their energy efficiency and endless hot water delivery. However, because our local mountain-fed ground water is incredibly cold in the winter, tankless units must be precisely sized with the correct BTU input to achieve the desired temperature rise. Traditional tanks are less expensive upfront but take up more physical space and cost more to operate long-term.

9. How often should I flush my traditional water heater tank in Colorado?

Due to the hard, mineral-heavy water supplied across the Front Range, traditional storage tank water heaters should be professionally flushed at least once a year. Regular flushing prevents sediment from baking into a solid scale crust at the base of the tank, preserving energy efficiency and extending the overall lifespan of your system.

10. Why is my tankless water heater struggling to deliver hot water during extreme winter cold?

During deep winter freezes, incoming municipal groundwater enters your home at near-freezing temperatures (35°F to 40°F). A tankless water heater must work much harder to raise that icy mountain water to your target comfort temperature. If your unit is undersized or hasn't been descaled recently, it will struggle to maintain proper GPM flow demands.

11. What is a water heater anode rod and when should it be replaced?

An anode rod is a sacrificial metal element designed to attract corrosive elements in the water, rusting away so that your steel water heater tank doesn't. In hard-water markets like Aurora and Castle Rock, these rods should be checked every 2 years and typically replaced every 3 to 5 years to keep the main tank from degrading.

12. Why is my water heater making a loud popping or cracking sound?

Loud popping, rumbling, or cracking sounds are clear indicators of severe sediment buildup. Water becomes trapped underneath a thick crust of mineral scale at the bottom of the tank. As the burner heats up, the trapped water boils, turns to steam, and violently bursts through the sediment layer. This causes severe thermal stress and shortens tank life.

Sump Pumps & Basement Drainage

13. What causes frequent sump pump failures during the Front Range spring thaw?

In the Denver Metro area, heavy winter snowpacks melt rapidly during the spring thaw, causing local water tables to rise aggressively. Sump pumps fail primarily due to mechanical fatigue from constant cycling, clogged intake screens caused by silt buildup, or float switches getting stuck against the basin wall. We specialize in high-output commercial cast-iron sump pump systems to keep basements completely dry.

14. Do I really need a battery backup system for my sump pump?

Yes, a battery backup is essential. Severe Front Range spring and summer storms routinely knock out local power grids right when heavy downpours or rapid snowmelt demand maximum performance from your pump. A secondary battery backup system ensures your finished basement remains protected during prolonged blackouts.

15. How long do sump pumps typically last in Colorado clay soils?

Builder-grade plastic sump pumps generally last between 3 to 5 years. Commercial-grade cast-iron submersible pumps installed by Just Plumbing last 7 to 10 years because the cast-iron housing rapidly dissipates heat during heavy continuous duty. Regular cleaning and checking the float switch helps maximize pump life.

Home Remodeling & Code Requirements

16. What is a thermal expansion tank and is it required by local building codes?

When water is heated inside a closed plumbing system (created by a PRV or backflow check valve), it expands in volume. A thermal expansion tank contains a pre-pressurized rubber bladder that absorbs this excess volume, protecting your pipes and water heater from pressure spikes past 150 PSI. Colorado municipal building codes require them on all new water heater installations.

17. What should my home's incoming water pressure be, and why does it matter?

Safe residential plumbing systems operate best between 50 to 65 PSI. If your municipal main pressure exceeds 80 PSI, it places ongoing hydraulic stress on pipe joints, copper lines, and flexible appliance hoses, triggering leaks and water hammer. Installing a calibrated Pressure Reducing Valve (PRV) resolves this issue permanently.

18. Can you update old plumbing lines during a kitchen or bathroom remodel?

Absolutely. A kitchen or bathroom remodel is the single best time to replace outdated galvanized or failing copper lines because the wall cavities are already open during demolition. Modernizing the hidden plumbing footprint via targeted partial repiping protects your custom tile and cabinetry from future leaks.

19. What is a frost-proof hose bibb and can it still freeze and burst?

A frost-proof hose bibb features a long internal stem that shuts off the water flow deep inside the warm insulation of your home's walls. However, if a homeowner leaves a garden hose attached during a freezing Colorado cold snap, water stays trapped inside the barrel. It will freeze, expand, and split the internal pipe wide open.

20. How do I locate my home's main water shutoff valve in an emergency?

In most Front Range homes, the primary indoor shutoff valve is located in the basement or crawlspace where the main water pipe enters through the foundation wall facing the street. It is typically marked by a bright blue or red ball valve lever or gate wheel directly adjacent to your municipal water meter.
